Cellular changes

    Cellular changes

    How cells adapt to stress, what happens when they cannot, and how the body responds.

    Cells adapt to stress until they can't

    Injury to cells and to their surrounding environment, the extracellular matrix, leads to tissue and organ injury.

    A normal cell works within a narrow range of structure and function, but it can adapt to physiologic demands or stress and so keep a steady state called homeostasis.

    Adaptation
    A reversible, structural, or functional response to both normal (physiologic) and adverse (pathologic) conditions.

    Like most of the body's adaptive mechanisms, cellular adaptations to adverse conditions are usually only temporarily successful.

    Severe or long-term stressors overwhelm the adaptive processes, and cellular injury or cellular death follows.

    An adapted cell is neither normal nor injured; it sits between the two states. If the stress is too severe or lasts too long, adaptation gives way to injury and then death.

    Cellular injury can be caused by any factor that disrupts cellular structures or deprives the cell of the oxygen and nutrients it needs to survive. Injury may be reversible (sublethal) or irreversible (lethal). It is classified broadly as chemical, hypoxic (lack of sufficient oxygen), free radical, unintentional or intentional, and immunologic or inflammatory.

    Cells adapt to escape and protect themselves from injury. Adaptations are reversible changes in cell size, number, phenotype, metabolic activity, or functions of cells.

    The most significant adaptive changes are atrophy (decrease in cell size), hypertrophy (increase in cell size), hyperplasia (increase in cell number), and metaplasia (reversible replacement of one mature cell type by another, less mature cell type, or a change in phenotype). Dysplasia (deranged cellular growth) is not a true adaptation but an atypical hyperplasia.

    Atrophy, hypertrophy, hyperplasia, metaplasia and dysplasia

    Four changes count as true adaptations. Dysplasia is related to them but is not one.

    Atrophy
    A decrease or shrinkage in cellular size. When enough of an organ's cells are atrophic, the whole organ shrinks.

    It can affect any organ but is most common in skeletal muscle, the heart, secondary sex organs, and the brain. It may be physiologic or pathologic.

    • Physiologic: occurs with early development. Example: the thymus gland atrophies during childhood.
    • Pathologic: follows decreases in workload, use, pressure, blood supply, nutrition, hormonal stimulation, or nervous stimulation. Prolonged bed immobilisation causes disuse atrophy of skeletal muscle.
    • Ageing: brain cells become atrophic, and endocrine-dependent organs such as the gonads shrink as hormonal stimulation decreases.

    Whether the cause is physiologic or pathologic, atrophic cells show the same basic changes.

    In muscle, ageing, disuse, dystrophy, systemic disease and ALS all tip the balance between protein synthesis and protein degradation toward degradation, and the muscle atrophies.

    Hypertrophy
    An increase in the size of cells, which increases the size of the affected organ.

    Much of what is known comes from studies of the heart. Heart and kidney cells are particularly responsive to enlargement. It can be physiologic or pathologic.

    • Physiologic: caused by increased demand, hormone stimulation (e.g., atrial natriuretic peptide), and growth factors (e.g., IGF-1). In skeletal muscle it follows heavy work and tends to diminish if the workload diminishes. Pregnancy is an example: hormone-induced uterine enlargement.
    • Pathologic: results from chronic hemodynamic overload, e.g., hypertension or heart valve dysfunction. Cardiac hypertrophy can progress to maladaptive states: dysrhythmias, heart failure, and sudden death.
    Hyperplasia
    An increase in the number of cells in an organ or tissue, resulting from an increased rate of cellular division.

    It occurs when injury has been severe and prolonged. The main mechanism is the production of growth factors, which stimulate the remaining cells (after cell loss or injury) to synthesise new cell components and ultimately divide. Another mechanism is increased output of new cells from tissue stem cells.

    • Compensatory (physiologic): lets certain organs regenerate. Removing part of the liver leads to hyperplasia of the remaining hepatocytes.
    • Hormonal (physiologic): mainly in estrogen-dependent organs such as the uterus and breast. After ovulation, estrogen stimulates the endometrium to grow and thicken. If pregnancy occurs, hormonal hyperplasia plus hypertrophy enlarges the uterus.

    Pathologic hyperplasia is abnormal proliferation of normal cells, from excessive hormonal stimulation or growth-factor effects on target cells. The most common example is endometrial hyperplasia, caused by an imbalance between estrogen and progesterone with relatively increased estrogen. It causes excessive menstrual bleeding; if growth-inhibition controls fail, the cells can undergo malignant transformation. Benign prostatic hyperplasia, from changes in hormone balance, is another example. In both, hyperplasia regresses if the hormonal imbalance is corrected.

    Dysplasia
    Abnormal changes in the size, shape, and organization of mature cells. Related to hyperplasia and often called atypical hyperplasia.

    Dysplastic changes are mostly found in epithelia, and the tissue architecture can be disorderly. Dysplasia is not cancer and may not progress to cancer. If it does not involve the entire thickness of the epithelium, it may be completely reversible once the inciting stimulus (e.g., certain hormonal stimuli) is removed in mild to moderate dysplasia.

    When dysplastic changes penetrate the basement membrane, the lesion is considered a preinvasive neoplasm, known as carcinoma in situ.
    Metaplasia
    The reversible replacement of one mature cell type (epithelial or mesenchymal) by another, sometimes less differentiated, cell type. It is found with tissue damage, repair, and regeneration.

    Sometimes the replacement cell suits the changed conditions better. Example: gastroesophageal reflux damages the squamous epithelium of the esophagus, and replacement by glandular epithelium may tolerate the acid better. Usually, though, the change is not beneficial.

    In a long-term cigarette smoker, chronic irritation replaces the normal ciliated columnar epithelial cells of the trachea and bronchi with stratified squamous epithelial cells. These do not secrete mucus or have cilia, so a vital protective mechanism is lost. Bronchial metaplasia can be reversed if the inducing stimulus, usually cigarette smoking, is removed. If the stimulus persists, it can initiate malignant transformation in the metaplastic epithelium.

    When injury becomes irreversible: necrosis and apoptosis

    In response to significant external stimuli, cell injury becomes irreversible and cells are forced to die. Cell death has historically been classified as necrosis and apoptosis.

    • Necrosis: rapid loss of plasma membrane structure, organelle swelling, mitochondrial dysfunction, and none of the typical features of apoptosis. Until recently only necrosis was considered passive or accidental, occurring after severe and sudden injury. It is the main outcome of several common injuries: ischemia, toxin exposure, certain infections, and trauma.
    • Apoptosis: a regulated or programmed cell process, characterised by "dropping off" of cellular fragments called apoptotic bodies.

    Necrosis looks different depending on the tissue and the cause. Choose a type below and compare.

    Coagulative necrosis
    Occurs mainly in the kidneys, heart, and adrenal glands. It commonly results from hypoxia caused by severe ischemia or by chemical injury, especially ingestion of mercuric chloride. Protein denaturation changes albumin from a gelatinous, transparent state to a firm, opaque state, like a cooked egg white. The tissue looks firm and slightly swollen, and the area is called an infarct.
    Liquefactive necrosis
    Commonly follows ischemic injury to neurons and glial cells in the brain. Brain cells are rich in digestive hydrolytic enzymes and lipids, and the brain has little connective tissue. The cells are digested by their own hydrolases, so the tissue turns soft, liquefies, and is walled off from healthy tissue, forming cysts. It can also result from bacterial infection (staphylococci, streptococci, Escherichia coli): hydrolases from the lysosomes of neutrophils liquefy bacterial and neighbouring tissue cells, which accumulate as pus.
    Caseous necrosis
    Commonly results from tuberculous pulmonary infection, particularly Mycobacterium tuberculosis. It is a combination of coagulative and liquefactive necrosis. Dead cells disintegrate, but hydrolases do not digest the debris completely. The tissue is soft and granular like clumped cheese, and a granulomatous inflammatory wall encloses it.
    Fat necrosis
    Occurs in the breast, pancreas, and other abdominal structures. Powerful lipases break down triglycerides, releasing free fatty acids, which combine with calcium, magnesium, and sodium ions to create soaps (saponification). The tissue looks opaque and chalk white.
    Gangrenous necrosis
    A term common in surgical practice: death of tissue from severe hypoxic injury, usually from arteriosclerosis or blockage of major arteries, especially in the lower leg. With hypoxia and later bacterial invasion the tissues can undergo necrosis. Dry gangrene is usually coagulative necrosis: the skin dries, shrinks and wrinkles, and turns dark brown or black. Wet gangrene develops when neutrophils invade, causing liquefactive necrosis, usually in internal organs. The site becomes cold, swollen, and black, with a foul odour from pus; if systemic symptoms become severe, death can ensue.
    Gas gangrene
    A special type caused by infection of injured tissue by one of many species of Clostridium. These anaerobic bacteria produce hydrolytic enzymes and toxins that destroy connective tissue and cellular membranes and form gas bubbles in muscle cells. It can be fatal if enzymes lyse the membranes of red blood cells, destroying their oxygen-carrying capacity. Death is the result of shock. Treatment is antitoxins plus supplemental oxygen in a hyperbaric (pressurized) chamber.

    First line of defense: physical, mechanical, and biochemical barriers

    Physical and mechanical barriers. The barriers against damage and infection are tightly associated epithelial cells: the skin, and the membranous sheets lining the gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and respiratory tracts. Mucosal epithelial cells are highly interconnected by junctions that stop microorganisms passing into the tissue underneath.

    • Normal cell turnover and "washing" of surfaces mechanically remove many microorganisms. Sloughing off and replacing dead skin cells also removes adherent bacteria.
    • Mechanical cleansing includes vomiting and urination.
    • Goblet cells of the upper respiratory tract make mucus that traps microorganisms; hairlike cilia move the mucus upward so it is expelled by coughing or sneezing.
    • Low temperature on the skin and low pH of the skin and stomach generally inhibit microorganisms, most of which prefer temperatures near 37°C (98.6°F) and a near-neutral pH.

    Biochemical barriers. Epithelial surfaces also make and secrete substances (epithelial-derived chemicals) that trap or destroy microorganisms. Mucus, perspiration (sweat), saliva, tears, and earwax can all trap and kill potential disease-causing microorganisms.

    • Sebaceous glands in the skin secrete antibacterial and antifungal fatty acids and lactic acid.
    • Perspiration, tears, and saliva contain lysozyme, an enzyme that attacks the cell walls of gram-positive bacteria.
    • These secretions make the skin surface acidic (pH 3 to 5), which is inhospitable to most bacteria.
    These physical and chemical barriers of the innate immune system are relatively static. Inflammation, the second line of defense, is programmed to respond to cellular or tissue damage.

    Second line of defense: inflammation

    Inflammation responds to cellular or tissue damage whether the damaged tissue is septic (contaminated with microorganisms) or sterile. It is a rapid, interactive system of humoral (soluble in the blood) and cellular systems that limits tissue damage, destroys contaminating microorganisms, initiates the adaptive immune response, and begins healing.

    1. It occurs in tissues with a blood supply (vascularized).
    2. It is activated rapidly, within seconds of damage.
    3. It depends on both cellular and chemical components, including plasma proteins.
    4. It is nonspecific: it happens in about the same way whatever the stimulus and whether or not the same stimulus has been met before.

    Vascular response. Virtually any injury to vascularized tissue activates inflammation. Causes include infection or necrosis (e.g., trauma, ischemia, nutrient deprivation, genetic or immune defects, chemical injury, foreign bodies, temperature extremes, ionizing radiation).

    Redness (erythema), heat, swelling (edema), and pain: the classic signs of acute inflammation, identified in the first century by the Roman writer Celsus. A fifth sign was added later: loss of function.

    Three characteristic changes appear in the microcirculation (arterioles, capillaries, venules) near the injury:

    1. Vasodilation (increased vessel size) slows blood velocity and increases blood flow to the injured site.
    2. Increased vascular permeability (vessels become porous as endothelial cells contract) and leakage of fluid (exudation) cause swelling (edema). As plasma moves out, blood becomes more viscous and flows more slowly; the increased blood flow and concentration of red cells cause locally increased redness (erythema) and warmth.
    3. White blood cells adhere to the inner vessel walls and migrate through enlarged junctions between endothelial cells into the surrounding tissue (diapedesis).

    Biochemical mediators (e.g., histamine, bradykinins, leukotrienes, prostaglandins) make the endothelial cells of capillaries and venules retract, opening spaces that let leukocytes and plasma into the tissue. The vascular changes deliver leukocytes (particularly neutrophils), plasma proteins, and other mediators to the injury, where they act together.

    The benefits of inflammation are:

    1. Prevents infection and further damage: fluid influx dilutes toxins from bacteria and dying cells; plasma protein systems (complement, clotting) help destroy and contain bacteria; neutrophils and macrophages "eat" cellular debris and infectious agents.
    2. Limits and controls the process: plasma protein systems (e.g., clotting), plasma enzymes, and cells (e.g., eosinophils) keep the response from spreading to healthy tissue.
    3. Engages the adaptive immune system for a more specific response to the pathogen(s), through macrophages and lymphocytes.
    4. Prepares the injury for healing: bacterial products, dead cells, and other products of inflammation are removed (through channels in the epithelium or by lymphatic drainage), and healing and repair begin.

    Lymphatic vessels drain extravascular fluid and debris. This also supports adaptive immunity: microbial antigens in lymph pass through the lymph nodes and activate both B and T lymphocytes. The lymphatic vessels may themselves become inflamed (lymphangitis), and the nodes (lymphadenitis) become hyperplastic, enlarge and often become painful.

    Acute versus chronic. Inflammation and repair divide into phases; the early (acute) response differs from the later (chronic) one in mediators and cells. The acute response is short and lasts only until the immediate threat is eliminated, usually 8 to 10 days from onset to healing.

    Superficially the difference is duration: chronic inflammation lasts 2 weeks or longer regardless of cause. Characteristic histologic and mechanistic differences may also be present.

    Chronic inflammation is sometimes preceded by an unsuccessful acute response. If bacterial contamination or foreign objects (dirt, wood splinters, glass) persist in a traumatic wound, the acute response may be prolonged beyond 2 weeks. Pus formation, suppuration (purulent discharge), and incomplete wound healing may characterise this type.

    It can also occur as a distinct process with little previous acute inflammation:

    • Mycobacteria (tuberculosis) have cell walls rich in lipid and wax, so phagocytes degrade them poorly and the acute response cannot clear them.
    • Microorganisms causing leprosy, syphilis, and brucellosis survive inside macrophages and avoid clearance.
    • Some microorganisms produce toxins that damage tissue and cause persistent inflammation even after the microorganism is killed.
    • Chemicals, particulate matter, or physical irritants (inhaled dusts, wood splinters, suture material) can cause prolonged inflammation.

    Chronic inflammation is characterised by a dense infiltration of lymphocytes and macrophages. If macrophages cannot limit the damage or infection, the body walls off and isolates the area, forming a granuloma. Granulomas may form when neutrophils and macrophages fail to destroy microorganisms in the acute response. Examples: some bacteria (Listeria sp., Brucella sp.), fungi (histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis), and parasites (leishmaniasis, schistosomiasis, toxoplasmosis); large antigen-antibody complexes, as in rheumatoid arthritis, can also cause them.

    Core definitions

    Physiology
    The function or mechanics of the body: the study of how living organisms function, including nutrition, movement, and reproduction.
    Pathology
    The study of diseases, especially the structural and functional changes they produce.
    Pathophysiology
    The physiology of abnormal states: the functional changes that accompany a particular syndrome or disease.
    Etiology
    The cause of a disease.
    Pathogen
    An agent of disease (e.g., bacteria, virus).
    Multifactorial
    Having many factors. Example: hypertension (family history, genetics, eating habits, stress).
    Iatrogenic
    Caused by treatment: disease caused by human intervention, such as an adverse reaction to medication.
    Idiopathic
    No known cause.
    Symptoms
    Feelings described by the patient: functional manifestations or evidence of a disease process (the patient's history).
    Signs
    Objective findings perceived by an examiner or physician, seen and measured by the doctor (the physical exam).
    Incidence
    New cases per unit of time.
    Prevalence
    New and old cases within a specific population per unit of time.
    Congenital disease
    Disease occurring at birth (related to genes and inheritance).
    Biopsy
    Excision of small amounts of living tissue for further evaluation and investigation.
    Autopsy
    Examination after death.
    Predisposing factors
    Tendencies that promote development of disease; they can include age, gender, inherited factors, and diet.
    Exacerbating
    Irritating or increasing symptoms (making them worse).
    Prevention
    Vaccines, dietary or lifestyle changes, removal of harmful materials, or stopping harmful activities like smoking. Primary prevention prevents something that has not happened yet; secondary prevention prevents the condition from occurring again.

    Onset can be:

    • Acute: sudden and obvious.
    • Chronic (insidious): gradual, with vague and very mild symptoms.
    • Acute disease: short-term illness that develops quickly (asthma, burn, heart attack).
    • Chronic disease: develops gradually over a long term, causing more damage (diabetes, hypertension, also asthma).
    Subclinical
    Pathologic changes occur, but there are no symptoms for a long time (diabetes, thyroid dysfunction, rheumatoid arthritis).
    Latent stage
    No clinical signs: the incubation period of a disease, with no symptoms (HIV, CMV, TB).
    Prodromal period
    Early in disease, with only general, non-specific symptoms (flu, psychological disorders).
    Remission
    A symptom-free period (cancer, hepatitis).
    Asymptomatic
    No noticeable symptoms (cancer, hepatitis, HIV, DM).
    Diagnosis
    The identifying label for a disease (lab examination, radiology, drugs).
    Prognosis
    The outcome (a guess).
    Morbidity
    Disease rate within a population.
    Mortality
    Death rate within a population.
    Infectious
    Caused by pathogenic microorganisms (bacteria, virus, fungus, parasite).
    Communicable
    Transmitted by agents, fomites, a vector, or a carrier.

Pathophysiology, Lecture 4

Endocrine diseases, part I

Thyroid, pituitary and adrenal disorders: hyperthyroidism, hypothyroidism, acromegaly, Cushing syndrome, hyperaldosteronism and adrenal insufficiency.

Thyroid axis

The hypothalamus releases thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), which acts on the pituitary. The pituitary releases thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), which acts on the thyroid. The thyroid releases triiodothyronine (T3) and thyroxine (T4). T3 and T4 act back on both TSH and TRH through a negative feedback loop.

T4 is converted to T3 by peripheral tissue. Only unbound (free) thyroid hormone is biologically active.

Hyperthyroid disorders

Hyperthyroid disorders are grouped under the term thyrotoxicosis. The lecture classifies them by cause.

  • Toxic diffuse goiter (Graves' disease): the most common hyperthyroid disorder. An autoimmune disorder in which thyroid-stimulating antibodies directed at thyrotropin receptors mimic TSH and stimulate T3 and T4 production.
  • Pituitary adenomas: produce excessive TSH secretion that does not respond to normal T3 negative feedback.
  • Toxic adenoma: a nodule in the thyroid, autonomous of the pituitary and TSH.
  • Toxic multinodular goiter (Plummer disease): several autonomous follicles that, if large enough, cause excessive thyroid hormone secretion.
  • Painful subacute thyroiditis: self-limiting inflammation of the thyroid gland caused by viral invasion of the parenchyma, resulting in the release of stored hormone.
  • Drug induced: for example, excessive exogenous thyroid hormone dosages or amiodarone therapy.

Graves' disease

Graves' disease is the underlying cause of 50% to 80% of cases of hyperthyroidism. Its cause is not known, but genetic factors interacting with environmental triggers play an important role in the pathogenesis. It is an autoimmune disease resulting from infiltration of lymphocytes and stimulation of the thyroid by autoantibodies directed against the TSH receptor.

These autoantibodies override normal negative feedback mechanisms. Stimulation of TSH receptors results in hyperplasia of the gland (goiter) and increased synthesis of thyroid hormone (TH), especially T3. Increased TH affects every physiologic system and produces the classic signs and symptoms of hyperthyroidism.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on elevated free T4 serum concentrations and suppressed TSH concentrations, except in TSH-secreting adenomas.

Clinical presentation

  • Weight loss or increased appetite
  • Lid lag
  • Heat intolerance
  • Goiter
  • Fine hair
  • Heart palpitations or tachycardia
  • Nervousness, anxiety, insomnia
  • Menstrual disturbances (lighter or more infrequent menstruation, amenorrhea) caused by hypermetabolism of estrogen
  • Sweating or warm, moist skin
  • Exophthalmos, also known as ophthalmopathy or thyroid eye disease

Therapeutics

  • Ablative therapy: common treatment for Graves disease, toxic nodule and multinodular goiter; radioactive iodine ablative therapy and surgical resection for adenomas, according to patient preferences or comorbidities. Ablative therapy often results in hypothyroidism.
  • Thyroidectomy
  • Antithyroid pharmacotherapy: methimazole, propylthiouracil.
  • Nonselective β-blockers (primarily propranolol; sometimes nadolol): for tremor and tachycardia.

The lecture's list runs a, b, c, e. It has no item d, so one treatment may be missing from it.

Thyroid storm

Thyroid storm is severe and life-threatening decompensated thyrotoxicosis. The mortality rate may be as high as 20%.

  • Precipitating causes: trauma, infection, antithyroid agent withdrawal, severe thyroiditis, postablative therapy (especially if inadequate pretreatment).
  • Presentation: fever, tachycardia, vomiting, dehydration, coma, tachypnea, delirium.
  • Pharmacotherapy: propylthiouracil, iodide therapy, beta blocker, corticosteroids.

Hypothyroid disorders

Classification

  • Hashimoto disease: the most common hypothyroid disorder in areas with iodine sufficiency. Autoimmune-induced thyroid injury results in decreased thyroid secretion. It disproportionately affects women.
  • Iatrogenic: thyroid resection or radioiodine ablative therapy for hyperthyroidism.
  • Iodine deficiency: the most common cause worldwide.
  • Secondary causes: pituitary insufficiency (failure to produce adequate TSH secretion, called by some a central or secondary hypothyroidism), and drug induced (for example, amiodarone, lithium).

Diagnosis

  • Low free T4 serum concentrations
  • Elevated TSH concentrations, typically seen as the first laboratory abnormality, usually greater than 10 mIU/L (normal or low if central hypothyroidism is the cause)
  • Thyroid antibodies such as antithyroid peroxidase and antithyroglobulin autoantibodies

Hashimoto disease

The most common cause of hypothyroidism in the United States is autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto disease, chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis), which results in gradual inflammatory destruction of thyroid tissue.

The tissue destruction involves infiltration of thyroid autoantibodies (antithyroid peroxidase and antithyroglobulin antibodies), autoreactive T lymphocytes, natural killer cells and inflammatory cytokines, and induction of apoptosis. Goiter formation is commonly observed due to inflammation but then gradually shrinks in size.

Clinical presentation

  • Cold intolerance
  • Dry skin
  • Fatigue, lethargy, weakness
  • Weight gain
  • Bradycardia
  • Slow reflexes
  • Coarse skin and hair
  • Periorbital swelling
  • Menstrual disturbances (more frequent or longer menstruation, painful menstruation, menorrhagia) caused by hypometabolism of estrogen
  • Goiter (primary hypothyroidism)

Myxedema

The characteristic sign of severe or long-standing hypothyroidism is myxedema, which results from altered composition of the dermis and other tissues. Connective fibers are separated by an increased amount of protein and mucopolysaccharides. This protein-mucopolysaccharide complex binds water, producing nonpitting, boggy edema, especially around the eyes, hands and feet and in the supraclavicular fossae.

The tongue and the laryngeal and pharyngeal mucous membranes thicken, producing thick, slurred speech and hoarseness.

Myxedema coma

Myxedema coma is severe and life-threatening decompensated hypothyroidism, with a mortality rate of 30%–60%.

  • Precipitating causes: trauma, infections, heart failure, medications (for example, sedatives, narcotics, anesthesia, lithium, amiodarone).
  • Presentation: coma (not required and uncommon, despite the terminology); altered mental state (very common); diastolic hypertension; hypothermia; hypoventilation.
  • Pharmacotherapy: intravenous thyroid hormone replacement, antibiotic therapy, corticosteroids.

Classification

The lecture focuses on the common anterior pituitary disorders.

Hypersecretory diseases

  • Acromegaly and gigantism: usually caused by a growth hormone (GH)-secreting pituitary adenoma.
  • Hyperprolactinemia: the most common cause is prolactinomas (prolactin-secreting pituitary tumor). It may also be drug induced (for example, serotonin reuptake inhibitors and some antipsychotics) or caused by central nervous system lesions.

Hyposecretory disease

  • GH deficiency: a congenital abnormality caused by GH gene deletion or GH-releasing hormone deficiency. Other causes are pituitary aplasia, head trauma and central nervous system infection. It may also be idiopathic.
  • Panhypopituitarism: the result of partial or complete loss of anterior and posterior pituitary function. It can be caused by primary pituitary tumor, ischemic necrosis of the pituitary, trauma from surgery, or irradiation. It results in adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) deficiency, GH deficiency, hypothyroidism and gonadotropin deficiency.

Acromegaly

Acromegaly results from continuous exposure to high levels of GH and IGF-1. It almost always is caused by a GH-secreting pituitary adenoma and rarely results from the ectopic production of GHRH. It usually occurs in adults in the 40–59-year-old age group, although it is often present for years before diagnosis because of the insidious nature of the disease.

Systemic complications are therefore present at diagnosis, leading to increased mortality and premature death. Deaths are caused by heart disease secondary to hypertension and coronary artery disease, stroke, diabetes mellitus, or malignancy (colon or lung cancers).

Systemic effects

Metabolism. GH has significant effects on glucose, lipid and protein metabolism. Hyperglycemia results from inhibition of peripheral glucose uptake and increased hepatic glucose production, followed by compensatory hyperinsulinism. Adipose tissue inflammation and impaired adipogenesis contribute to insulin resistance. Diabetes mellitus occurs when the pancreas cannot secrete enough insulin to offset the effects of GH.

Cardiovascular system. The associated pathophysiology is not clearly understood, but hypertension and left heart failure are seen in one-third to one-half of individuals with acromegaly. Cardiomyopathy associated with progressive and unrestrained myocardial growth is a significant factor. Valvular heart disease and arrhythmias can occur.

Kidney. GH acts on the renal tubules to increase phosphate reabsorption, leading to mild hyperphosphatemia.

Pituitary. Because the adenoma increasingly becomes a space-occupying lesion, hypopituitarism may occur because of compression of surrounding hormone-secreting cells.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on failure of an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) to suppress GH serum concentrations, with elevated IGF-1. GH serum concentrations alone are unreliable, given the pulsatile pattern of release in the body.

Clinical presentation

The disease has a slow onset, and many symptoms do not appear for years.

  • Excessive sweating
  • Osteoarthritis, joint pain, paresthesias, or neuropathies
  • Coarsening of facial features
  • Increased hand volume or ring size, increased shoe size
  • Hypertension, heart disease, cardiomyopathy
  • Sleep apnea
  • T2D

Treatment

Surgical resection of the tumor; drugs such as dopamine agonists (bromocriptine, cabergoline) and a somatostatin analog (octreotide).

Adrenal cortex

The zona glomerulosa produces mineralocorticoids (aldosterone), the zona fasciculata produces cortisol, and the zona reticularis produces sex hormones (testosterone/estradiol). The medulla, in the centre, produces catecholamines.

Pituitary adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) stimulates the zona fasciculata and the zona reticularis. The renin-angiotensin system (RAS) stimulates the zona glomerulosa. Cortisol and sex hormones act back on pituitary ACTH through negative feedback.

Cushing syndrome

Cushing syndrome comprises the hypersecretory cortisol diseases. They are classified by whether the excess depends on ACTH.

  • ACTH-dependent: the result of excessive ACTH secretion. Causes are pituitary corticotroph adenoma (Cushing disease) and ectopic ACTH syndrome (extrapituitary tumor).
  • ACTH-independent: the result of excessive cortisol secretion or exogenous steroids. Causes are unilateral adrenocortical tumors, bilateral adrenal hyperplasia or dysplasia, and exogenous steroid administration.

Diagnosis

The presence of hypercortisolism is established through 24-hour urinary free cortisol concentration. The etiology must then be differentiated, because it is key to treatment options:

  • Plasma ACTH concentrations (normal or elevated in ACTH-dependent disease)
  • Pituitary magnetic resonance imaging (Cushing syndrome vs. ectopic ACTH syndrome)

Clinical presentation

  • Central obesity and facial rounding, quite common
  • Peripheral obesity and fat accumulation
  • Myopathies
  • Osteoporosis, back pain, compression fracture
  • Abnormal glucose tolerance or diabetes
  • Amenorrhea and hirsutism in women
  • Lower abdominal pigmented striae (red to purple)
  • Hypertension (principal cause of morbidity and mortality)

Mechanisms

Glucose. Glucose intolerance occurs because of cortisol-induced insulin resistance and increased gluconeogenesis and glycogen storage by the liver. Overt diabetes mellitus develops in approximately 20% of individuals with hypercortisolism. Polyuria is a manifestation of hyperglycemia and resultant glycosuria.

Muscle. Protein wasting is caused by the catabolic effects of cortisol on peripheral tissues. Muscle wasting leads to muscle weakness and is especially obvious in the muscles of the extremities, with thinning of the limbs.

Bone. Loss of the protein matrix and increases in bone resorption lead to osteoporosis and can result in pathologic fractures, vertebral compression fractures, bone and back pain, kyphosis and reduced height. Hypercalciuria may result in renal stones, experienced by approximately 20% of individuals with this disease.

Skin and small vessels. Loss of collagen leads to thin, weakened integumentary tissues through which capillaries are more visible, and the tissues are easily stretched by adipose deposits. Together these changes account for the characteristic purple striae, most often observed in the truncal area. Loss of collagenous support around small vessels makes them susceptible to rupture, leading to easy bruising, even with minor trauma. Thin, atrophied skin is easily damaged, leading to skin breaks and ulcerations.

Pigmentation. Bronze or brownish hyperpigmentation of the skin, mucous membranes and hair occurs when there are very high levels of ACTH. It is caused by increased levels of melanocyte-stimulating hormones resulting from excess conversion of proopiomelanocortin when ACTH concentration is elevated.

Cardiovascular system. With elevated cortisol levels, vascular sensitivity to catecholamines is increased significantly, leading to vasoconstriction and hypertension. Metabolic syndrome with abdominal obesity, hypertension, glucose intolerance and dyslipidemias is a common complication. Individuals with hypercortisolism are at increased risk for cardiovascular complications (CAD, heart failure and stroke).

Immune system. Chronically elevated cortisol levels cause suppression of the immune system, increased susceptibility to infections and poor wound healing.

Mental status. Approximately 50% of individuals with hypercortisolism experience alterations in their mental status, caused by the effects of cortisol on hippocampal neurons and the subsequent implications on learning, memory and other neurologic functions. These may range from irritability and depression to severe psychiatric disturbances, such as schizophrenia, which improve with treatment.

Androgens. Females may experience symptoms of increased adrenal androgen levels: increased hair growth (especially facial hair), acne and oligomenorrhea. Rarely do androgen levels become high enough to cause changes of the voice, recession of the hairline and hypertrophy of the clitoris unless an adrenal carcinoma is involved. Infertility is more common among women.

Therapeutics

  • If excessive exogenous corticosteroid use is causative, discontinue or minimize use, tapering doses gradually to avoid sudden withdrawal due to hypothalamus pituitary adrenal axis suppression.
  • Surgical resection of the causative area or tumor is the usual treatment of choice.
  • Pharmacotherapy is usually reserved on the basis of the same criteria listed earlier for pituitary adenomas; ketoconazole is an antifungal and it hinders cortisol production. The lecture does not itemize those criteria.

Hyperaldosteronism

Primary aldosteronism

The lecture classifies primary aldosteronism as bilateral adrenal hyperplasia (70% of cases) and aldosterone-producing adenoma (30% of cases).

Its pathophysiology text states that primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn syndrome, primary aldosteronism) is usually the result of a unilateral benign aldosterone-producing adrenal adenoma, and that bilateral adrenal nodular hyperplasia and adrenal carcinomas account for the remainder of cases.

These two statements disagree about which cause is the more common, and the lecture does not reconcile them. Both are given here as written.

The incidence is estimated to be about 10% of all hypertensive individuals; however, approximately 33% of people with resistant hypertension will have evidence of primary hyperaldosteronism.

Excessive autonomous secretion of aldosterone without its principal regulator, angiotensin II, causes hypokalemia and induces insulin resistance; promotes inflammation, endothelial dysfunction and cardiovascular remodeling (increased left ventricular wall and carotid intima thickness); and affects adipose tissue differentiation and function.

Primary hyperaldosteronism can therefore influence the features of the metabolic syndrome, including hypertension (most common cause of secondary hypertension), obesity, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance and hyperglycemia.

Diagnosis and clinical presentation

  • Elevated plasma aldosterone/renin ratio (normal ratio is less than 25)
  • Other features: hypernatremia, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, glucose intolerance
  • Clinical presentation (can be asymptomatic): hypertension, muscle weakness or fatigue, headache, polydipsia, nocturnal polyuria

Therapeutics: spironolactone (drug of choice).

Secondary hyperaldosteronism

Secondary hyperaldosteronism (secondary aldosteronism) involves excessive aldosterone secretion from the action of angiotensin II through a renin-dependent mechanism. Examples include decreased circulating blood volume (for example, in dehydration, shock or hypoalbuminemia) and decreased delivery of blood to the kidneys (for example, renal artery stenosis, heart failure or hepatic cirrhosis).

Here, the activation of the renin-angiotensin system and subsequent aldosterone secretion may be seen as compensatory, although in some instances (for example, congestive heart failure) the increased circulating volume further worsens the condition.

Adrenal insufficiency

Classification

  • Primary adrenal insufficiency (Addison disease): caused by autoimmune disorder, infection or infarction. It results in cortisol, aldosterone and androgen deficiencies.
  • Secondary adrenal insufficiency: caused by exogenous steroid use (from chronic suppression), whether oral, inhaled, intranasal or topical, and by surgery, trauma, infection or infarction. It results in impaired androgen and cortisol production.

Diagnosis and clinical presentation

The lecture focuses on Addison disease. An abnormal rapid cosyntropin (synthetic ACTH) stimulation test, with a blunted increase in cortisol concentrations, suggests adrenal insufficiency.

  • Hyperpigmentation (caused by elevated ACTH concentrations)
  • Weight loss
  • Dehydration
  • Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, elevated blood urea nitrogen

Therapeutics: steroids replacement.

Pathophysiology of Addison disease

Addison disease is characterized by inadequate corticosteroid and mineralocorticoid synthesis and elevated serum ACTH levels (loss of negative feedback). Before clinical manifestations of hypocortisolism are evident, more than 90% of total adrenocortical tissue must be destroyed.

Idiopathic Addison disease (organ-specific autoimmune adrenalitis) causes adrenal atrophy and hypofunction and is an organ-specific autoimmune disease. Autoantibodies and autoreactive T cells specific to adrenal cortical cells are present in 50% to 70% of individuals with idiopathic Addison disease, with proliferation of immunocytes directed against specific antigens within the adrenocortical cells.

The adrenal glands in idiopathic Addison disease are smaller than normal and may be misshapen.

Summary

  • In primary thyroid disease, TSH moves opposite to free T4. In a TSH-secreting adenoma and in central hypothyroidism it does not.
  • Acromegaly is diagnosed by failure of an OGTT to suppress GH, with elevated IGF-1, because GH alone is unreliable.
  • In Cushing syndrome, plasma ACTH helps differentiate the etiology, which is key to treatment options.
  • The plasma aldosterone/renin ratio is normally less than 25.
  • Addison disease causes cortisol, aldosterone and androgen deficiencies, with elevated ACTH. Secondary adrenal insufficiency results in impaired androgen and cortisol production.

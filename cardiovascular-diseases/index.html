Pathophysiology, Lecture 2

Cardiovascular diseases

Deep venous thrombosis, hypertension, atherosclerosis, myocardial infarction, heart failure and dysrhythmias.

Deep venous thrombosis

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) includes deep venous thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). DVT is a blood clot that remains attached to a vessel wall, usually on a single side of a lower extremity. A detached thrombus is a thromboembolus. Venous thrombi are more common than arterial thrombi because flow and pressure are lower in the veins than in the arteries.

Triad of Virchow

Three factors, termed the triad of Virchow, promote venous thrombosis:

  • Venous stasis, associated with immobility, obesity, prolonged leg dependency, age and congestive heart failure (CHF).
  • Venous intimal damage, related to trauma, venipuncture and IV medications.
  • Hypercoagulable states, arising from inherited disorders, smoking, malignancy, liver disease, pregnancy, oral contraceptives, hormone replacement, hyperhomocysteinemia and antiphospholipid syndrome.

Accumulation of clotting factors and platelets leads to thrombus formation in the vein, often near a venous valve. Inflammation around the thrombus promotes further platelet aggregation, and the thrombus grows proximally.

Risk

Virtually everyone who is hospitalized is at significant risk for DVT, especially those with orthopedic trauma or surgery, spinal cord injury, age older than 60 years and obstetric/gynecologic conditions. Individuals with malignancy (especially ovarian and pancreatic cancer) and women who are pregnant are also at significant risk.

Outcome

Most thrombi eventually dissolve without treatment, but untreated DVT is associated with a high risk of thromboembolization: part of the clot travels from the leg to the lung, resulting in a pulmonary embolism.

In up to one-third of individuals with DVT, persistent venous outflow obstruction may lead to post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS), characterized by chronic, persistent pain, edema and ulceration of the affected limb.

Clinical manifestations

Clinical manifestations of DVT are often absent. If a symptom is present, it is typically pain. Other signs include unilateral leg swelling, dilation of superficial veins, calf tenderness, and skin that is mottled or cyanotic. Because DVT is usually asymptomatic and difficult to detect clinically, prevention of DVT is a high priority.

Prevention and treatment

The patient should be mobilized as soon as possible after illness, injury or surgery.

  • Low risk: additional prophylactic treatment can include aspirin or pneumatic devices.
  • Higher risk: prophylaxis with low-molecular-weight heparin or, in some cases, direct thrombin inhibitors.
  • VTE occurs: the same drugs used for prophylaxis can be used for treatment, but with different dosage regimens.

Hypertension

Approximately 95% of cases of hypertension have no known cause and are therefore diagnosed as primary hypertension, also commonly called essential hypertension. Secondary hypertension accounts for 5% of cases and is associated with an underlying primary disorder, such as renal disease.

Hypertension is a complex disorder that affects the entire cardiovascular system. All types and stages are associated with increased risk for target organ disease events, such as myocardial infarction (MI), kidney disease and stroke.

Pathophysiology

Hypertension is caused by increases in cardiac output, total peripheral resistance, or both. Cardiac output is increased by any condition that increases heart rate or stroke volume. Peripheral resistance is increased by any factor that increases blood viscosity or reduces vessel diameter (vasoconstriction).

Primary hypertension

A combination of genetic and environmental factors is thought to be responsible. Genetic predisposition is polygenic, including polymorphisms associated with renal sodium excretion, insulin and insulin sensitivity, activity of the sympathetic nervous system (SNS) and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), and cell membrane sodium or calcium transport. Epigenetic links between environmental factors, such as diet, exercise and smoking, and gene expression are also being defined.

Risk factors include age, ethnicity, family history of hypertension and genetic factors, lower education and socioeconomic status, tobacco use, psychosocial stressors, sleep apnea, and dietary factors: dietary fats, higher sodium intake, lower potassium intake and excessive alcohol intake.

Glucose intolerance (diabetes mellitus) and obesity are also significant risk factors. Many of these are risk factors for other cardiovascular disorders. Hypertension, dyslipidemia and glucose intolerance are often found together in a condition called metabolic syndrome.

Secondary hypertension

Secondary hypertension is caused by an underlying disease process that raises peripheral vascular resistance or cardiac output. Examples include renal vascular or parenchymal disease, adrenocortical tumors, adrenomedullary tumors (pheochromocytoma), and drugs (oral contraceptives, corticosteroids, antihistamines).

Blood pressure returns to normal if the cause is identified and removed before permanent structural changes occur.

Pathway to sustained hypertension

Genetics and environment lead, through three linked factors, to vasoconstriction and renal salt and water retention. These give increased peripheral resistance and increased blood volume, and both lead to sustained hypertension, which causes organ disease directly and through vascular remodeling.

Complicated hypertension

Complicated hypertension is chronic hypertension that damages the walls of systemic blood vessels. Within the walls of arteries and arterioles, smooth muscle cells undergo hypertrophy and hyperplasia with associated fibrosis of the tunica intima and media, a process called vascular remodeling.

Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is a form of arteriosclerosis in which thickening and hardening of the vessel are caused by the accumulation of lipid-laden macrophages within the arterial wall, which leads to the formation of a lesion called a plaque.

It is not a single disease but a pathologic process that can affect vascular systems throughout the body, resulting in ischemic syndromes that vary widely in severity and clinical manifestations. It is the leading cause of coronary artery and cerebrovascular disease.

Plaque formation

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) enters the arterial intima through an intact endothelium. Inflammation and oxidized LDL cause endothelial cells to express adhesion molecules that bind monocytes and other inflammatory and immune cells. Monocytes penetrate the vessel wall and become macrophages. Lipid-laden macrophages are called foam cells. Foam cells accumulate and form the fatty streak, and many inflammatory cytokines and enzymes are released that injure the vessel wall.

  • Damaged endothelium. The wall consists of endothelium, tunica intima, tunica media and adventitia. Response to injury involves monocytes, platelets, macrophages and lipids.
  • Fatty streak. Platelets attach to the endothelium, a foamy macrophage ingests lipids, smooth muscle migrates into the intima, lipid accumulates, and a fibroblast appears.
  • Fibrous plaque. A collagen cap of fibrous tissue, with fibroblasts, a lipid pool, and a fissure in the plaque.
  • Complicated lesion. A thrombus over a thinning collagen cap and lipid pool, filling much of the lumen.

Plaque rupture

Many plaques are “unstable,” meaning they are prone to rupture even before they affect blood flow, and are clinically silent until they rupture. Rupture occurs because of the inflammatory activation of proteinases (matrix metalloproteinases and cathepsins), apoptosis of cells within the plaque, and bleeding within the lesion (plaque hemorrhage).

Plaques that have ruptured are called complicated plaques. Once rupture occurs, exposure of underlying tissue results in platelet adhesion, initiation of the clotting cascade, and rapid thrombus formation that may suddenly occlude the affected vessel, resulting in ischemia and infarction. Aspirin or other antithrombotic agents are used to prevent this complication.

Risk factors for coronary artery disease

Conventional or major nonmodifiable risk factors for coronary artery disease (CAD) are advanced age, male gender or women after menopause, and family history. Aging and menopause are associated with increased exposure to risk factors and poor endothelial healing. Family history may contribute through genetics and shared environmental exposure, and many gene polymorphisms have been associated with CAD and its risk factors.

Major modifiable conventional risks are dyslipidemia, hypertension, cigarette smoking, diabetes and insulin resistance, obesity and sedentary lifestyle, and an atherogenic diet.

If individuals receive appropriate preventive care, modification of these factors can significantly reduce the risk for CAD.

Ischemic heart disease and myocardial infarction

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) divides into stable angina/chronic disease and ACS. ACS divides into unstable angina, STEMI and NSTEMI.

Beyond the lectureThe lecture does not expand these abbreviations. In standard use, ACS is acute coronary syndrome, STEMI is ST-elevation myocardial infarction and NSTEMI is non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction.

When coronary blood flow is interrupted for an extended period, myocyte necrosis occurs. This results in MI. Pathologically there are two major types of MI: subendocardial infarction and transmural infarction. Clinically, MI is categorized as STEMI or non-STEMI.

In each, a thrombus lies in a coronary artery. Unstable angina shows a zone of ischemic myocardium, non-STEMI a narrow band of damage next to the endocardium, and STEMI a broader band extending further through the wall towards the pericardium.

Electrocardiogram

On a normal electrocardiogram (ECG) the P wave represents atrial depolarization, the Q, R and S deflections represent ventricular depolarization, and the T wave represents ventricular repolarization.

Electrocardiographic alterations associated with ischemia are ST-segment depression, T-wave inversion and ST-segment elevation.

Cellular injury

Cardiac cells can withstand ischemic conditions for about 20 minutes before cellular death takes place. After only 30 to 60 seconds of hypoxia, ECG changes are visible. Even if cells are metabolically altered and nonfunctional, they can remain viable if blood flow returns within 20 minutes.

After 8 to 10 seconds of decreased blood flow, the affected myocardium becomes cyanotic and cooler. Myocardial oxygen reserves are used very quickly, within about 8 seconds after complete cessation of coronary flow. Glycogen stores decrease as anaerobic metabolism begins. Glycolysis can supply only 65% to 70% of the total myocardial energy requirement and produces much less ATP than aerobic processes.

Hydrogen ions and lactic acid accumulate. Because myocardial tissues have poor buffering capabilities and myocardial cells are very sensitive to low cellular pH, these products further compromise the myocardium. Acidosis may make the myocardium more vulnerable to the damaging effects of lysosomal enzymes and may suppress impulse conduction and contractile function, thereby leading to heart failure.

Oxygen deprivation is also accompanied by electrolyte disturbances, specifically loss of potassium, calcium and magnesium from cells. Myocardial cells deprived of necessary oxygen and nutrients lose contractility, diminishing the heart's pumping ability.

Ischemic myocardial cells release catecholamines (epinephrine and norepinephrine), predisposing the individual to serious imbalances of sympathetic and parasympathetic function and to irregular heartbeats (dysrhythmia).

Cellular death

After about 20 minutes of myocardial ischemia, irreversible hypoxic injury causes cellular death and tissue necrosis. Necrosis releases intracellular enzymes, such as troponin, through the damaged cell membranes into the interstitial spaces. The lymphatics pick up the enzymes and transport them into the bloodstream, where they can be detected by serologic tests.

Heart failure

Heart failure (HF) is the pathophysiologic condition in which the heart is unable to generate an adequate cardiac output such that inadequate perfusion of tissues or increased diastolic filling pressure of the left ventricle, or both, occurs. Consequently, pulmonary capillary pressures are increased.

An estimated 5.7 million Americans ≥20 years of age have HF, and it causes 1 in 9 deaths in the United States. HF is a pressing, world-wide problem, with millions of people experiencing worsening heart failure. Ischemic heart disease and hypertension are the most important predisposing risk factors, with 75% of HF cases occurring in individuals with hypertension.

Other risk factors include age, smoking, obesity, diabetes, renal failure, valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathies, myocarditis, congenital heart disease and excessive alcohol use.

Left-sided heart failure

Causes of systolic dysfunction are coronary artery disease/ischemic heart disease, dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertension and valvular heart disease.

Causes of diastolic dysfunction are hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, restrictive cardiomyopathy and hypertension.

Ejection fraction (EF) is the volume ejected divided by the volume the ventricle filled with. A ventricle filled with 100 ml that ejects 60 ml has an EF of 60%. Normal EF is 50% to 70%.

In systolic dysfunction, a ventricle filled with 100 ml ejects only 30 ml, an EF of 30%. In diastolic dysfunction, a ventricle filled with only 50 ml ejects 30 ml, an EF of 60%: the ejected volume is the same, but the EF lies in the normal range.

Left-sided heart failure produces pulmonary edema: fluid in the lungs, filling part of an air sac.

Right-sided heart failure

Causes are left-sided heart failure and chronic lung diseases.

Neurohumoral processes

Systolic congestive heart failure is characterized by a complex constellation of neurohumoral, inflammatory and metabolic processes.

Myocardial dysfunction arises from myocardial infarction, ischemic heart disease, hypertension or other causes, and activates two limbs that converge on ventricular remodeling.

In the RAAS limb, renin acts with angiotensinogen from the liver to give Ang I, and the lungs give Ang II, which leads to aldosterone and vasoconstriction.

  • Catecholamines. Sympathetic nervous system activation initially compensates for a decrease in cardiac output by increasing heart rate and peripheral vascular resistance. However, catecholamines cause numerous deleterious effects on the myocardium: direct toxicity to myocytes, induction of myocyte apoptosis, myocardial remodeling, downregulation of adrenergic receptors, facilitation of dysrhythmias, and potentiation of autoimmune effects on the heart muscle.
  • Angiotensin II (Ang II). Activation of the RAAS increases preload and afterload and also causes direct toxicity to the myocardium. Ang II mediates remodeling of the ventricular wall, contributing to sarcomere death, loss of the normal collagen matrix and interstitial fibrosis. This leads to decreased contractility, changes in myocardial compliance and ventricular dilation.
  • Aldosterone. Aldosterone causes salt and water retention by the kidney and also contributes to myocardial fibrosis, autonomic dysfunction and dysrhythmias. It has also been implicated in endothelial dysfunction and prothrombotic effects.
  • Arginine vasopressin. Arginine vasopressin, also known as antidiuretic hormone, causes both peripheral vasoconstriction and renal fluid retention. These actions exacerbate hyponatremia and edema in heart failure. The arginine vasopressin type 2 antagonist tolvaptan is indicated for heart failure that is resistant to conventional diuretics.

Dysrhythmias

A dysrhythmia, or arrhythmia, is a disturbance of heart rhythm. Normal heart rhythms are generated by the SA node and travel through the heart's conduction system, causing the atrial and ventricular myocardium to contract and relax at a regular rate appropriate to maintain circulation at various levels of physical activity.

Dysrhythmias range in severity from occasional “missed” or rapid beats to serious disturbances that impair the pumping ability of the heart, contributing to heart failure and death. They can be caused by either an abnormal rate of impulse generation by the SA node or other pacemaker, or by the abnormal conduction of impulses through the heart's conduction system, including the myocardial cells themselves.

Their pathophysiology, diagnosis and treatment are highly complicated; atrial fibrillation is the lecture's example.

Atrial fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac rhythm disorder. It occurs when electrical impulses in the heart become disorganized, leading to a rapid and irregular heart rhythm. People with AF have a four- to fivefold increased risk of stroke and a two- to threefold increased risk of heart failure.

Efficient pumping relies on the coordinated activity of the atria and ventricles, which involves contraction of cardiomyocytes in response to membrane depolarization. Electrical impulses begin in the sinoatrial (SA) node and first propagate through the atria, then pass through the atrioventricular (AV) node, which delays their transit, and enter the ventricles, causing sequential cardiomyocyte contraction.

In AF, the electrical activity in the atria is rapid and irregular. As a result, there is no coordinated atrial contraction and, depending on the filtering effect of the SA node, the response of the ventricles can also be rapid and irregular.

Beyond the lectureThe lecture attributes the filtering effect to the SA node. Standard references attribute it to the AV node. Confirm the wording with the lecturer.

On the ECG, the SA node fires before the P wave. The P-wave marks activation and contraction of the atria, and the QRS marks activation of the ventricles. PR and T are also marked.

After the last normal beat, atrial electrical activity becomes irregular and atrial contraction ineffective. The QRS complexes show a rapid irregular ventricular response and impaired pump function.

Because 12-lead ECG is not cost-effective on a large scale, future population screening for AF might involve oscillometry, smartphone cameras or hand-held ECG rhythm strips.

Management of atrial fibrillation

Treatment of AF involves strategies to achieve a normal heart rate, restore normal rhythm and reduce the risk of stroke, which is the most important priority for AF management. Heart rate is controlled with rate-controlling drugs such as β-blockers. Rhythm control uses antiarrhythmic drugs, for example amiodarone.

Ineffective blood pumping raises the likelihood of coagulation and thrombosis. As a result, patients with AF have an increased risk of stroke and often require oral anticoagulant therapy.

Summary

  • The triad of Virchow promotes venous thrombosis; untreated, a thrombus can embolize to the lung.
  • Hypertension arises from raised cardiac output, total peripheral resistance, or both.
  • Unstable plaques can rupture before they affect blood flow, and may suddenly occlude the vessel.
  • Myocardial cells can remain viable if blood flow returns within 20 minutes.
  • Ejecting 30 ml gives an EF of 30% in systolic dysfunction and 60% in diastolic dysfunction.
  • In AF, ineffective pumping raises the risk of thrombosis and stroke.
